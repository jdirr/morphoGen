# Laser architecturing: materials, stiffness and cracks

*A literature survey for [the architectures at the scale of the spot](laser.md#14-architectures-at-the-scale-of-the-spot): which materials a local laser treatment turns into two-phase sheets, whether it can change Young's modulus, how local property gradients steer and blunt ductile cracks, and the state of the art.*

*Literature survey, 8 November 2026. Scope: through-thickness treatment of 0.5–2 mm sheets with one beam or two aligned beams, smallest treated feature ≈1 mm × 1 mm, sheets up to several tens of centimetres. Every reference was found during this search. [nv] marks an item that could not be fully verified (author list, volume or value read from a secondary record). "Estimate" marks an order-of-magnitude calculation of this survey, not a literature value.*

Starting point (PIMM): Wang's thesis [1] and papers [2, 3]. Note on [2]: Mater. Sci. Eng. A 831, 142205 is the AISI 430 study (laser hardening and laser-assisted carburizing; fully multi-track treated samples gain +60% in yield stress and +45.8% in UTS). The corrugated martensite tracks in ductile ferritic steel are reported in [3].

**Synthesis.** The large through-thickness contrasts come from transformations that complete within a laser dwell: martensite in hardenable C–Mn–B steels (×2.6–2.9 in strength, with the reverse contrast in ductility and fracture work), reversion of strain-induced martensite in cold-rolled metastable austenitic steels (YS from ≥ 965 MPa down to 300–1000 MPa depending on peak temperature, elongation 9% → ≈40%, plus a magnetic contrast), and composition or anneal changes in NiTi (functional contrast). Precipitation-hardened alloys (Al, maraging, Cu–Be, β-Ti) show large furnace contrasts, but ageing takes hours: a laser can only dissolve, hence soften, locally. In Al alloys the dissolved state re-ages at room temperature (7075 within 10 h [29], 6xxx T4 through natural ageing [28]) and T6 overageing is stable but limited to ≈−30–35%. AA2024-T3 combines the drawbacks: little to dissolve beyond natural-ageing clusters that re-form, modest overageing (≤20% hardness loss), hot cracking on melting and high diffusivity; its documented laser benefit is residual stress for crack retardation [32]. For thickness, the relevant time is the diffusion time across the (half) thickness, L²/4a: for L = 1 mm, ≈0.04 s in steel, ≈0.08 s in Ti-15-3, ≈0.004 s in Al (estimate). Steels, Ti and NiTi therefore keep features of ≈1 mm with through-thickness treatment, while Al and Cu spread heat laterally as fast as through the thickness, consistent with the ≈5 mm softened zones reported in 6063 [28]. Double-sided heating halves L and divides this time by four.

## 2. Can a local laser treatment change Young's modulus? (Q2)

**Table 2.** Young's modulus of phases and laser-treated states.

| System | Values | Notes | Ref. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ferrite–pearlite vs martensite (SAE 1050) | Martensite E 3.2% lower, G 3.6% lower | Resonant ultrasound; α-iron 0.6–1.3% above F–P | [49] |
| Austenitic vs ferritic stainless steel | Most austenitic grades 193–200 GPa (301 ≈193), ferritic ≈200 GPa | γ ↔ α′ changes E by a few % | [50] |
| Texture in steel sheet | "High Young's modulus" sheet: ≥ 205 GPa (RD, 45°), ≥ 220 GPa (TD) by γ-fibre texture | Laser re-austenitization alters local texture: expected change ≤ 5–10% (estimate) | [51] |
| Nitrides and carbides in steel | γ′-Fe4N 159 ± 17 GPa [nv]; cementite 177–200 GPa (experimental values compiled in [53]) | Nitrided and carburized layers are not stiffer than ferrite | [52, 53] |
| Laser-nitrided Ti-6Al-4V | 177 vs 114 GPa (layer vs substrate, as reported) | Surface layer only; cracking | [41, 42] |
| Metastable β Ti-15-3 | Solution treated 82 GPa; aged 107–111 GPa (+30–35%) | α precipitation; through-thickness possible in 0.4–2.4 mm strip | [34, 35] |
| Ti-29Nb-13Ta-4.6Zr | 60–100 GPa depending on oxygen and ageing; UTS 600–1400 MPa | α precipitation raises E | [54] |
| Ti-V, Ti-Nb, Ti-V-Sn | Athermal or stress-induced ω raises E; minima 51–57 GPa when ω is suppressed | – | [55] |
| ω-Ti (single phase, HPT) | E and G of ω above both α and β | ω is the stiffest Ti phase | [56] |
| Aged β-Ti, laser surface treatment | Laser-melted β layer: lower E and hardness whatever the ageing; without melting, ω on cooling can cancel the drop | Only direct demonstration found of a laser-induced stiffness gradient in a structural alloy | [37, 38] |
| NiTi | B2 austenite ≈67–70 GPa; B19′ martensite in tension ≈25 GPa (literature 28–41); diffraction moduli of martensite ≈120 GPa | Low martensite value is a tension-only effect of texture and anisotropy; switchable locally by laser [43] | [57, 43] |
| Fe78Si9B13 metallic glass ribbon, laser-crystallized stripes | Modulus +20–30% (cited), density +3.1% | 24 µm ribbons; crystallization eigenstrain programs 3D buckling | [58] |
| Laser-hardened low-C plates | Reported "stiffness" gains (+5% flexural, +8–21% axial in analytical estimates) | Plastic origin (higher yield of the layer), not a modulus change | [10] |

**Synthesis.** In steels, Young's modulus is nearly phase-independent: martensite is 3% below ferrite–pearlite, austenite within a few % of ferrite, nitrides (Fe4N) are softer, cementite comparable. Texture can move in-plane E by up to ≈5–10% (estimate from [51]), and the stiff compounds of laser alloying stay in thin surface layers. A modulus architecture in steel is therefore not realistic; reported stiffness gains in laser-hardened steel plates are post-yield effects. Two systems offer a real, through-thickness modulus contrast. Metastable β-Ti: aged 107–111 GPa vs solution treated 82 GPa (+30–35%), with ω able to raise E further, and a published laser-induced stiffness gradient [37, 38]. The macroscopic effect is nevertheless small: for a 50/50 laminate of 82 and 110 GPa, the Voigt and Reuss bounds are 96 and 94 GPa, a ≈2% anisotropy (estimate), so the architecture acts on local load paths rather than on effective elasticity. NiTi: 67–70 GPa (B2) vs ≈25–40 GPa (B19′, tension) at room temperature, switchable locally [43]; the same bounds give 47.5 vs 36.8 GPa, ≈30% anisotropy (estimate), with a nonlinear, temperature-dependent response. In steels, an elastic architecture has to be geometric (thickness modulation, corrugation, laser forming) or based on eigenstrains: the transformation strain of laser tracks creates residual stresses and distortion that can program buckled or multistable shapes, as shown with crystallized stripes in metallic glass ribbons [58]; no such demonstration in steel sheets was found.

## 3. Steering and blunting cracks with local property gradients (Q3)

**Table 3.** Evidence and tools for crack control in heterogeneous metals.

| Topic | Key result | Notes | Ref. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength mismatch in HAZ-notched welds | Overmatch with moderately tough HAZ: crack deviates toward the softer material; embrittled HAZ: overmatch lowers critical CTOD through constraint | Deep-notch geometry dominates constraint | [59] |
| Laser-welded Al6056 (undermatched fusion zone) | Crack started in the HAZ kinks into the softer fusion zone; GTN reproduces the path, a cohesive model needs a prescribed path | GTN to set limits for cheaper 2D cohesive runs | [60] |
| Micromechanical models for welds | GTN and related models predict higher initiation and growth resistance of HAZ, constraint effects | Review, steels and Al | [61] |
| Configurational forces | Material inhomogeneity term C_inh adds to J_far: shielding or anti-shielding | Elastic and elastic–plastic | [62, 63] |
| Periodic stiffness variation | Design rules for flaw-tolerant multilayers (bone, nacre, sponges) | Requires strong elastic contrast | [64] |
| Soft interlayers (yield stress inhomogeneity) | Driving force drops strongly once the crack has crossed a soft interlayer: arrest without delamination; design variables: interlayer yield strength, thickness, spacing | Elastic–ideally plastic analyses; steel laminates (X210CrW12 or C45 matrix, DC04 interlayers) arrest the crack in the first interlayer | [65–67] |
| Soft-to-hard transitions | Fatigue crack coming from the softer steel slows and stops before the interface; from the harder side it passes | Explosion-clad ferritic/austenitic steel | [68] |
| Fracture work contrast by laser tempering (22MnB5) | w_e 38 (martensite), 130 (laser-tempered), 212 kJ/m² (F–P) | Toughness ratio 3.4–5.6 within one sheet | [4] |
| Laser-hardened tracks, fatigue | Fatigue life ×2–4; temporary arrest at track/matrix borders | 0.2 C and 0.45 C steels | [69] |
| Laser heating lines, residual stress | Strong retardation in AA2198-T8; +285% crack growth life in 2 mm AA2024-T3 | Compressive field between lines | [70, 32] |
| Functionally graded materials | Phase-field FGM model identifies gradient profiles that prevent unstable fracture; ductile FGM study links plastic strain to crack paths and arrest | Brittle [71]; ductile [72] | [71, 72] |
| Nacre-like and lamellar metals | Mg–Ti composites: brick-and-mortar is the least tough (K at Δa = 1 mm: 31.8 vs 43.5 MPa√m for crossed-lamellar), cracks penetrate the bricks; heterogeneous lamella Ti: 3× CG strength at equal ductility | Weak interfaces or strong contrast needed for deflection | [73, 74] |
| Damage and fracture models | Rice–Tracey void growth; GTN; Johnson–Cook cumulative damage; review of continuum models; phase-field ductile fracture; element erosion vs phase-field convergence | – | [75–81] |

**Synthesis.** Three robust rules emerge. (i) Under strength mismatch, ductile cracks drift toward the softer material [59, 60], and a soft zone placed across the path shields the crack once crossed [65–67], whereas a crack running from hard to soft material is anti-shielded [62, 63, 68]; laser tempering or reversion lines in a hard matrix provide exactly the interlayer variables of Sistaninia and Kolednik (yield strength, width, spacing), in sheet form, with toughness ratios of 3.4–5.6 measured in 22MnB5 [4]. (ii) Hard zones bring low local toughness and constraint: hard tracks crossing a crack path in a soft matrix delay fatigue cracks at their borders [69] but can trigger cleavage if embrittled [59]. (iii) Brick-and-mortar layouts need weak interfaces: without them cracks cut through the bricks [73]; for laser architectures with moderate contrast and no interfaces, continuous soft lines across the expected path (laminate or crossed-lamellar type) are more promising than bricks. Residual-stress patterns from laser heating lines are a second, documented lever for fatigue [32, 70]; no study of monotonic ductile tearing in laser-architectured sheets was found, which leaves the field open. Modelling: an efficient chain is (a) stationary-crack screening of layouts with J and C_inh computed from graded property maps [62, 63]; (b) growth simulations with a regularized model, either nonlocal GTN, which captures path deviation in welds [60, 61], or phase-field ductile fracture [79, 80], which handles graded properties without remeshing [71, 72]; uncoupled Rice–Tracey or Johnson–Cook criteria with element erosion [75, 77] are cheap but give mesh-biased paths [81]; (c) calibration per phase on notched and shear specimens (triaxiality and Lode dependence [78]), 3D solids with several elements through the thickness to capture slant fracture (estimate), and validation by DIC and crack-path tracking.

**Synthesis.** The field has moved from formability-driven tailoring (tailor heat treated Al blanks, soft zones in press-hardened parts) to architecture-driven patterning (PIMM, Kim and Yoon, TU Delft, recent 304 stripes). The exploited contrast is still almost always strength in steels, and the targets are necking strain, formability and crash energy. Elastic and fracture-oriented design remain open, as do non-ferrous alloys beyond Al.

## 5. Recommendation for a demonstrator

1. **Cold-rolled metastable austenitic stainless steel (301/301LN full hard, 0.5–1.5 mm) with laser reversion tracks.** Expected contrast: YS ≥ 965 MPa (full hard) vs 300–1000 MPa (reverted, tunable by peak temperature), elongation ≈9% vs ≈40%, plus a ferromagnetic/paramagnetic contrast that makes the architecture visible by magnetic imaging during loading (TRIP). Why: reversion completes in 1–100 s at 700–1000 °C with no quench and no hardenability limit; laser reversion and patterning are demonstrated [14–19]. Check grain growth at high peak temperature and the evolution of the contrast by TRIP.
2. **Three-level C–Mn–B architecture (22MnB5 or MS1500-type, 1–2 mm): ferrite–pearlite, laser-tempered martensite, martensite**, for the crack-steering demonstrator. Expected: UTS 594/920/1523 MPa and w_e 212/130/38 kJ/m² [4], arranged as soft lines across the crack path following the interlayer rules [65–67]. Why: process windows already mastered at PIMM [1–3, 12], largest combined strength and toughness contrast.
3. **Aged metastable β-Ti strip (Ti-15-3, 0.4–2.4 mm) with laser re-solution tracks.** Expected: YS 965–1172 → ≈770 MPa, elongation 5–7% → 22%, E 107–111 → 82 GPa [34, 35]. Why: the only structural alloy found with a through-thickness modulus contrast (±30%) and a published laser stiffness gradient [37, 38]; low conductivity keeps 1 mm features. To verify first: α dissolution at laser time scales, oxygen pick-up (shielding on both faces), ω in the heat-affected zone.
4. **NiTi strip (0.3–0.5 mm), local laser annealing of cold-rolled strip or Ni-depletion pulses**, for an elastic/functional demonstrator: apparent modulus ≈25–40 vs 67–70 GPa at room temperature, local Ms raised up to ≈65 °C so that treated zones are martensitic and the base austenitic [43, 57], with superelastic, shape memory and elastic domains in one sheet [44–46]. Limits: cost, sheet size, melting needed for Ni depletion.

Not recommended for architecture: AA2024 (low contrast, cracking; residual-stress patterning only), Al 6xxx/7xxx (≤ ≈35% and re-ageing), Mg (surface effects only), Cu–Be until laser data exist.

**Elastic architecture.** In steels, a modulus architecture is not realistic (contrast ≤ 3–5% by phase, ≤ 5–10% by texture); an elastic architecture in steel must be geometric or eigenstrain-based. A material stiffness architecture is realistic in β-Ti (local ±30%, but ≈2% macroscopic anisotropy for a 50/50 laminate, estimate) and in NiTi (≈2.7× apparent contrast, ≈30% anisotropy, nonlinear and temperature dependent, estimate). For a first elastic demonstrator, NiTi is the most convincing, β-Ti the most structural.
